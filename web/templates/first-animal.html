OUR FIRST ANIMAL · A PROPOSED START

Many animals.
One careful beginning.

Parrots, dogs, cats, frogs… the tools welcome them all. For our first study, we propose one parrot species: the budgerigar.

A candidate, not a settled scientific verdict.

This is an editorial recommendation, conditional on access to existing companion-animal households and an avian research collaborator. No participants have been recruited. A cockatiel cohort could be more practical if that is where we have access; dogs are a fallback.

Why start with a small parrot?

Repeated observations of a familiar animal could help connect sounds with independently observed behavior. Budgerigars also have a research starting point in learned contact calls and individual vocal signatures. That gives us a narrower question than “translate every animal.”

Parrots’ ability to imitate people is fascinating, but learned human words and natural calls need separate labels. A sound resembling “hello” does not establish what a parrot intends.

PROVISIONAL FIRST CHOICE

Budgerigars

One species, repeat observations and published contact-call research. We still need usable household access, independent behavior labels and a specialist-reviewed protocol. Overlapping flock calls will be a challenge.

ACCESS COULD DECIDE

Cockatiels or other parrots

A strong existing community could make a separate cockatiel study more feasible. Do not pool parrot species simply because they are parrots. African greys would need their own study and evidence review.

PRACTICAL ALTERNATIVES

Dogs and cats

Both deserve support in the app. Dog research offers a speech-model transfer baseline; cat recordings illustrate useful context labels and reuse restrictions. Breed, individual, household and recording conditions can confound results.

A WIDER RESEARCH HORIZON

Frogs, farm animals and beyond

Existing corpora help us study mixed callers and context labels. Fish, insects and bats may need underwater, vibration or ultrasonic equipment. A phone microphone cannot measure every animal’s signals.

The first useful question

Does a sound add information about visible behavior on an animal and household the model has never seen? Begin by checking whether independent people can agree on a few observable labels. If they cannot, model training is premature.

  1. Agree on one species, a small behavior codebook and an approved passive observation protocol.
  2. Observe normal routines. Keep uncertain identity, overlapping callers and unknown behavior explicit. Never provoke calls, isolate an animal or play sounds to elicit a response.
  3. Keep related animals, households, devices and sessions together when separating training and evaluation data. Compare simple baselines before larger models.
  4. Publish limitations and unsuccessful results too. A behavior prediction does not by itself demonstrate meaning or conversation.
